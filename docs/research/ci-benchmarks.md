# Benchmarks in CI: state of the art

Research note for task 1.1 of `ci-benchmarks`. It feeds the A/B mode of `micro` (design D2), spike S12 on the runners' noise (D3), the harness's reduced plan and its ratios (D4), the workflow's outputs and trend (D5) and its cost (D6). Every claim was read from the pinned sources at the end on 2026-10-15, and versions are the latest released that day. Library and platform facts come from current documentation: Context7 for criterion, critcmp, Bencher, GitHub Actions and GitHub's REST API. Context7 indexes CodSpeed only through its pytest plugin and indexes neither github-action-benchmark, the Android emulator runner action nor the runner images, so for those the note reads the vendor's documentation site, the repository's README at the pinned commit and GitHub's billing pages, and says so in each section. It builds on `docs/research/microbenchmarks.md`, which surveyed the harnesses and the continuous-benchmarking services from a quiet host's side; what that note found is not repeated here, only what changes on a shared runner.

Out of scope: choosing the harness (archived design D13 of `microbenchmarks`), profilers, and physical devices in CI, which need a self-hosted runner and, on iOS, a person at the phone for its approval prompt (proposal, What Changes).

## Findings in brief

- **Two builds measured on one machine, interleaved, are what a shared machine can compare.** Laaber, Scheuner and Leitner measured microbenchmarks on AWS, GCE and Azure at coefficients of variation from 0.03% to over 100%, by benchmark and instance type, and found that running test and control on the same instances in randomized order detects slowdowns of 10% or less with high confidence. Abedi and Brecht's randomized multiple interleaved trials (RMIT) answer the same problem, periodic effects that bias consecutive trials, and duet benchmarking, both artifacts at once on one machine, improved accuracy 2.3 to 12.5 times on ScalaBench and DaCapo and 23.8 to 82.4 times on SPEC CPU 2017. Bencher calls the idea relative continuous benchmarking. The pairing cancels what both sides share, the machine above all, not what varies within the job.
- **criterion compares runs inside one target directory.** `--save-baseline` and `--baseline` judge one run against one stored run with a t-test and a 1% noise threshold, in prose, at exit status 0 (`docs/research/microbenchmarks.md`); `--baseline-lenient` tolerates missing benchmarks and `--load-baseline` compares two stored sets without measuring. cargo-criterion "does not currently support baselines" and prints JSON messages with `--message-format=json`; its last release is 1.1.0, of 2021-07-28. critcmp compares two saved baselines or exported JSON files with a `-t` threshold in percent; its last release is 0.1.8, of 2023-07-12. None runs rounds or alternates the sides.
- **GitHub's standard runners.** 4 vCPUs and 16 GB on `ubuntu-latest` in a public repository, 2 vCPUs and 8 GB in a private one; 3 M1 cores and 7 GB on `macos-latest` and on the `xcode-27` preview image in both. Each job gets a new VM, but not the same CPU: CodSpeed found `ubuntu-24.04` jobs on an AMD EPYC 7763 in 9 of 10 runs and an Intel Xeon Platinum 8370C in 1, whose larger caches changed glibc's choice of `malloc` and flagged 8 unrelated benchmarks. Two runs therefore differ by machine as well as by code, while the two sides of an A/B in one job share the machine. MooBench on GitHub's runners detected changes down to 4.41% in sequential workloads (Reichelt, Jung and van Hoorn); Bencher puts the variance between runs on shared runners at over 30%.
- **The hosted services.** CodSpeed counts simulated cycles under its Valgrind fork, one run per benchmark, or times on bare-metal macro runners; it is free for open source and up to 5 users, and $15 a user a month beyond, and it wants a run on every push to the default branch for its baseline. Bencher stores what adapters parse, applies statistical thresholds and rents bare-metal runners at $1.00 an hour on its $100-a-month Pro plan; its free plan takes public projects only. github-action-benchmark keeps a history on a `gh-pages` branch and alerts on a ratio to the previous result, 200% by default. Each is an account, a token or a branch, and each builds its history from per-push runs, which this project rules out.
- **The Android emulator.** Standard Linux runners have offered KVM since 2024-04-02, enabled by a udev rule, and ReactiveCircus/android-emulator-runner v2.38.0 installs the image, creates and boots the AVD and runs a script beside it. arm64 macOS runners support no nested virtualization, so no accelerated emulator runs there. API 36 has a `google_apis` x86_64 image; the measured AVD is arm64, rendering on the host GPU.
- **Simulators.** `macos-latest` is the macOS 26.6.2 image with Xcode 26.0.1 to 26.6, 26.6 by default, and the iOS 26.2, 26.4 and 26.5 runtimes, an iPhone 17 among their devices; the `xcode-27` preview image has Xcode 27.0 by default, 27.1 and a 27.2 beta, with the iOS 27.0 runtime only. The Simulator driver types keys and text through dtuhidd, which needs Xcode 27's CoreSimulator; under Xcode 26 its input is touch only, and that path has never run (`crates/simulator/README.md`, Compatibility). Neither image has the validated pair, Xcode 27.0 with iOS 26.5.
- **Triggers and outputs.** A schedule runs the default branch's latest commit, in UTC, can be delayed or dropped at the start of an hour, and is disabled in a public repository after 60 days without activity. `workflow_dispatch` takes up to 25 inputs, `choice` among their types, once the file is on the default branch. A step's job summary takes up to 1 MiB; artifacts are kept for the repository's retention, 90 days by default; the REST API lists a workflow's runs by event and status and a run's artifacts, and serves an artifact as a zip.
- **Prices, since 2026-01-01.** $0.006 a minute on a 2-core Linux runner and $0.062 on a 3- or 4-core macOS runner, each job rounded up to the minute; plans' included minutes (2,000 on Free, 3,000 on Pro and Team) are consumed at list price, and public repositories pay nothing. The weekly schedule, both suites, comes to about $6.60 a week, $29 a month, in a private repository, 86% of it macOS; a dispatch of the micro suite alone to about $1.90 (estimate below).
- **The threshold question.** With both sides on one machine and interleaved, the sources put reliable detection near 5 to 10%: 10% or less in three clouds with randomized order, 4.41% on GitHub's runners for sequential work. A 10% default is defensible until S12 measures `main` against `main` on both runner kinds; the quiet host's 5%, twice criterion's 1.15% CV in spike S8, is not.

## Comparing two builds

### criterion's baselines, cargo-criterion and critcmp

Context7 (`/criterion-rs/criterion.rs`, which now indexes the moved repository, and `/burntsushi/critcmp`); the book at `82b191e`.

**Does.** A run of `cargo bench` stores each benchmark's samples and estimates under `$CRITERION_HOME/<id>/new/` and compares them with the previous run's copy in `base/`. `--save-baseline <name>` compares with the named set and overwrites it, `--baseline <name>` compares without overwriting, `--baseline-lenient <name>` does the same without failing on benchmarks the set lacks ("useful for CI"), and `--load-baseline <name>` takes a stored set as the new data, so two stored sets compare without measuring again; the book's own workflow saves `master`, checks out the branch and measures again in the same tree. The comparison is one run against one run. The FAQ's answer to running criterion in CI is "You probably shouldn't (or, if you do, don't rely on the results)", because virtualization "introduces a great deal of noise"; it recommends Iai's instruction counts and describes the same procedure for any tool: build main, run once for a baseline, switch to the branch, build and run again. cargo-criterion runs criterion benchmarks with its own analysis, prints a JSON message per benchmark with `--message-format=json` (typical, mean, median, MAD and slope, each with bounds, and a `change` against the previous run) and "does not currently support baselines". critcmp reads baselines from `target/criterion/`, or files written with `--export`, and prints each benchmark's times side by side, `-t 5` hiding differences under 5%.

**For this project.** A comparison of two refs needs two trees, built with their own target directories, which a named baseline joins only through one shared `$CRITERION_HOME`; criterion would then judge one pair of runs per benchmark at its 1% noise threshold and say so in prose. `micro` already reads each run's `new/estimates.json` under a fresh `$CRITERION_HOME` (design D15). Rounds of fresh homes, base and head in turn, give it those files per side and round, judged by `micro`'s own rule, which is what D2 keeps from criterion: the per-run files, not the comparison.

### Interleaved A/B runs

The papers are read through their abstracts and the authors' summaries, cited below: Laaber et al. through the abstract and talk, Abedi and Brecht through Scheuner's thesis, duet benchmarking and MooBench through their arXiv abstracts.

- **Laaber, Scheuner and Leitner (2019)** ran Java and Go microbenchmarks from four projects on three instance types in each of AWS, GCE and Azure and on bare metal, over 4.5 million data points. Variability "differs substantially between benchmarks and instance types (by a coefficient of variation from 0.03% to >100%)", and "executing test and control experiments on the same instances (in randomized order)" detects slowdowns "of 10% or less with high confidence". Their talk's detection simulation gives the share of configurations that reliably detected each slowdown: testing the versions together in one batch ("batch testing"), 97% at 5% and 100% at 10%; testing each version at its own time ("version testing"), 15% at 5%, 23% at 10% and 88% at 20%.
- **Abedi and Brecht (2017)**, randomized multiple interleaved trials: single trials and multiple consecutive trials, the common practice, can lead to erroneous conclusions in clouds with periodic effects; interleaving the alternatives, in a random order each round, spreads those effects over all of them.
- **Bulej et al. (2020)**, duet benchmarking: both artifacts run in parallel on one shared machine and are judged by their relative performance, which improved accuracy 2.3 to 12.5 times, 5.03 on average, for ScalaBench and DaCapo, and 23.8 to 82.4 times, 37.4 on average, for SPEC CPU 2017.
- **Reichelt, Jung and van Hoorn (2024)** compared MooBench's overhead measurements on GitHub's runners with three bare-metal machines: bare metal had lower runtimes and standard deviation for multi-threaded work, but "performance changes up to 4.41% are detectable by GitHub actions, as long as only sequential workloads are examined". Its follow-up traced a regression MooBench showed there to a Linux kernel change on the runners.
- **Tools.** Bencher's relative continuous benchmarking measures base and head in one job; tango runs two executables sample by sample in lock-step (`docs/research/microbenchmarks.md`).

**For this project.** `micro --ab` interleaves whole runs of the suites, base and head within each round with the order alternating between rounds, and judges each benchmark by the median of each side's round medians. A run of the 62 Linux benchmarks takes about ten minutes, so a benchmark's base and head medians are minutes apart: coarser than tango's samples or duet's simultaneous runs, and the alternation and the median over rounds are what keep a drift through the job from landing on one side. Per-benchmark interleaving through criterion's filter, two processes per benchmark per round, is the finer form, to try if S12 finds the rounds too coarse.

CodSpeed's simulation is the one that would remove the runner's noise: the count does not move with load or with the VM. Its numbers are not wall time, it needs Valgrind and so Linux, its criterion layer replaces the dependency, and its blog post shows that even counts move with the runner's CPU model, by 1.6% for one benchmark, through glibc's CPU-dependent `malloc`. CodSpeed logs the CPU to explain such moves and offers its bare-metal runners against them.

**For this project.** None is adopted: each needs an account, a token or a branch written by CI, and each expects runs on pushes, which the maintainer rules out. The summary and the artifacts carry the results, and an artifact chain carries the trend (D5). CodSpeed's simulation instrument stays D2's alternative should S12 find no threshold under 15%.

|---|---|---|---|
| `ubuntu-latest` | 4 vCPUs, 16 GB, x64 | 2 vCPUs, 8 GB, x64 | Ubuntu 24.04 |
| `macos-latest`, `macos-26` | 3 M1 cores, 7 GB, arm64 | the same | macOS 26.6.2 |

Each standard runner is "a new virtual machine (VM) hosted by GitHub"; only the 1-CPU `ubuntu-slim` shares a VM. The documentation says nothing of performance variability. The CPU behind a label varies from job to job (CodSpeed, above), and an arm64 macOS runner supports no nested virtualization, "a limitation of Apple's Virtualization Framework". A job runs for at most 6 hours.

**For this project.** The micro A/B's guard keeps its busy-process check, and its load bound becomes the runner's core count, 2 to 4: on a VM the load average counts the job's own setup, and the host's bound of 4 means nothing on 2 or 3 cores (D2). The trend compares runs on different VMs, possibly on different CPUs, so it is shown and never judged (D5).

### KVM and the Android emulator

GitHub's changelog of 2024-04-02 extended hardware-accelerated Android virtualization, until then on larger runners, to the standard 2-vCPU Linux runners, once the runner's user may open `/dev/kvm`: the step writes `KERNEL=="kvm", GROUP="kvm", MODE="0666", OPTIONS+="static_node=kvm"` to `/etc/udev/rules.d/99-kvm4all.rules`, then `udevadm control --reload-rules` and `udevadm trigger --name-match=kvm`. The README of ReactiveCircus/android-emulator-runner (v2.38.0 of 2026-07-05, read at `a421e43`) recommends `ubuntu-latest` over macOS, "2-3 times faster", with that step first. The action installs the SDK packages for `api-level`, creates the AVD from `target`, `arch` and `profile`, boots it with `emulator-options`, by default `-no-window -gpu swiftshader_indirect -no-snapshot -noaudio -no-boot-anim`, waits up to `emulator-boot-timeout` (600 s) for the boot, runs `script` and kills the emulator. `arch` defaults to `x86`; API 36's image is `system-images;android-36;google_apis;x86_64` (also `aosp_atd`, `google_atd` and Play Store variants), read from Google's repository manifest.

**For this project.** The emulator job runs API 36 `google_apis` on x86_64 with KVM and software rendering, unlike the measured arm64 AVD rendering on the host GPU (`docs/research/emulator-frames.md`), so its tap-to-frame numbers are not the gated span's; with the gRPC controller on (`-grpc 8554 -grpc-use-token`), taps and frames take the path the 10.1 run measured with the controller on (`crates/android/README.md`).

### Simulators on macOS runners

From the images' READMEs. `macos-latest` (macOS 26.6.2, image 20260907.0351.1) has Xcode 26.0.1, 26.1.1, 26.2, 26.3, 26.4.1, 26.5 and 26.6, the default, with Simulator runtimes iOS 26.2, 26.4 and 26.5 and devices such as iPhone 17, 17 Pro and 17e. `xcode-27` (macOS 27.0.1, image 20261006.0244.1) has Xcode 27.0, the default, 27.1 and a 27.2 beta, with the iOS 27.0 runtime and devices such as iPhone 17 and iPhone 18 Pro. Both carry Java 21 and Node; `brew` installs libjpeg-turbo, as CI's macOS leg does for the Simulator driver's encoder.

**For this project.** The Simulator driver supports Xcode 26.0 up to, not including, 28, but types keys and text only through dtuhidd, which Xcode 27's CoreSimulator offers: under Xcode 26, input would be touch only, and nothing has run there (`crates/simulator/README.md`, Compatibility). The login flow fills two fields, so the Simulator job needs the `xcode-27` image, on its iOS 27.0 runtime, which no run has validated; the job's report records the runtime in its provenance, and a failure there is a finding rather than a measurement.

### Triggers, summaries, artifacts and the previous run

- **`schedule`.** "Scheduled workflows run on the latest commit on the default branch", in UTC unless a time zone is set, at most every 5 minutes. Runs "can be delayed during periods of high loads", "High load times include the start of every hour", and "some queued jobs may be dropped", so GitHub advises another minute of the hour. In a public repository, scheduled workflows are disabled after 60 days without activity.
- **`workflow_dispatch`.** Up to 25 inputs and 65,535 characters of them; `choice` resolves to a string. It triggers only once the file is on the default branch; after one run it can be dispatched against any branch or tag through the API or `gh workflow run`.
- **Job summaries.** `$GITHUB_STEP_SUMMARY` takes Markdown; each step may add up to 1 MiB, more fails that step's summary upload with an annotation and not the step, and a job shows up to 20 steps' summaries.
- **Artifacts.** `actions/upload-artifact` (v7.0.2) takes `retention-days`, which cannot exceed the repository's or organization's limit; artifacts and logs are kept 90 days by default, a public repository allowing 1 to 90 and a private one 1 to 400.
- **The previous run.** `GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/actions/workflows/{workflow_id}/runs` filters by `event` (`schedule`), `status` (`completed`, `success`, ...) and `branch`; `GET /repos/{owner}/{repo}/actions/runs/{run_id}/artifacts` lists a run's artifacts and `GET .../actions/artifacts/{artifact_id}/zip` downloads one, `gh api` sending the job's `GITHUB_TOKEN` with `actions: read`.

## What we take

- **D1.** Triggers `schedule` and `workflow_dispatch` only. The schedule's minute moves off the hour, to 02:17 UTC on Sundays, since GitHub names the start of every hour as the time when scheduled runs are delayed or dropped. A first, cheap job resolves the refs to commits and finds the previous scheduled run.
- **D2.** Rounds of fresh `$CRITERION_HOME`s, base and head alternating, judged by the median of each side's round medians against the threshold alone, with allocations exact; criterion's per-run files, not its comparison. The guard's load bound is the runner's core count.
- **D3.** 10% until S12, which measures `main` against `main` on both runner kinds: the sources support 5 to 10% only with both sides on one machine.
- **D4.** The Simulator job on the `xcode-27` image, whose CoreSimulator gives the driver keys and text, rather than `macos-latest`; the emulator job on `ubuntu-latest` with KVM, API 36 `google_apis` x86_64 through android-emulator-runner, with the controller on and software rendering. Maestro from its release archive at a pinned version, checked against its SHA-256, and agent-device from npm at a pinned version, through Socket Firewall as local installs are; WebDriverAgent's adapter drives physical iPhones only, so on the Simulator it is skipped with its note, as on a host.
- **D5.** Tables in `$GITHUB_STEP_SUMMARY`, well under 1 MiB, `report.json` and `report.md` as artifacts for 90 days, and the previous scheduled run's artifact fetched through the REST API, compared, and printed beside the values, never judged.
- **D6.** The estimate above, with the macOS jobs as the cost.
- **Reused.** `just micro`'s guard, reader of criterion's files and allocation lines, exact allocation comparison, its rule for an entry without an interval and its reports; the harness's adapters, golden checks, hand-offs, provenance and reports.

## Where this design differs

- From the services: no account, no token, no branch written by CI, no runs on pushes; the history is a chain of artifacts, 90 days long, and the trend across runs is shown, not judged, because runs land on different VMs and CPUs.
- From `just micro` on a quiet host: two refs on one machine instead of one run against a committed baseline measured elsewhere, the threshold alone without the baseline's interval, and a threshold of 10% rather than 5%; the committed baseline stays the local judgement.
- From the harness on a measured host: one fixture per platform, 10 iterations after 2 warm-up, and no gate; mobile-agent is judged by how many times faster its p50 is than each baseline's on the same flow in the same run, the factor the README's tables give.
- From the literature: interleaving by whole suite runs rather than by sample or by simultaneous runs, at three rounds.

## Risks

- **Noise past 10% on a runner.** The alignment-sensitive decodes, whose medians followed the stack's layout on this host (archived design D16 of `microbenchmarks`), and the three-core macOS VM are the likely sources. S12 sets the threshold per runner kind; without one under 15%, the micro job reports without failing and CodSpeed's simulation is the next step.
- **The Simulator job's runtime and image.** iOS 27.0 on the `xcode-27` preview image has not run the driver, and preview images change; the job may find a failure rather than a number.
- **Time.** The Simulator job's estimate, 70 minutes, is near its 90-minute bound, Maestro's flows two thirds of it.
- **The emulator is not the measured one.** x86_64 with software rendering on a VM, against arm64 with the host GPU: its ratios compare tools on the same emulator, not the gated spans.
- **Drift of the baselines and images.** Maestro and agent-device are pinned in the workflow, each bump a line here; the images move under the labels, and the reports' provenance records Xcode, the OS and the runtime.
- **Schedules.** Delayed or dropped at busy times, and disabled after 60 days without activity in a public repository; a dispatch measures the same way.
- **Billing.** Nothing runs until the account's payments are fixed or the repository is public.
