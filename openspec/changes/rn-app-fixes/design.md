# Design

## Context

See proposal.md. The smoke test ran the maintainer's React Native app (Expo, a development client) on an iPhone 17 Simulator with iOS 26.x through the CLI built from main at 14:53 on 2026-10-09, driven by another agent session from `help agent` alone; its report lists every command with its outcome, the listings' sizes, the verdicts and the timing spans, and reproduces each defect. What the foundation change built that these fixes extend: the mirror's modal cut on iOS (task 10.22, `cover_by_modal_views`), the Android drawing-order cut (task 10.28, `cover_by_views_on_top` from the agent's `on_top` flag), `visibility` and `scroll_viewport` (10.15, 10.16), `fill`'s read-back with its hold after typed keys (10.23, 10.30) and its targeted re-read (10.29), the keyboard window the Simulator's reader bounds by what it draws (10.21), and the listing rules for React Native fields and labelled generic nodes (7.11, 7.12). The relevant spike numbers: S3 measured the Simulator keyboard's slide-in at about 1.2 s before its benchmark typed, and the Simulator's typed entry of 36 characters at 199 ms once the keyboard was up (10.31).

## Goals / Non-Goals

**Goals:**
- An agent cannot act on what it cannot see: a covered element is cut from the listing and refused, and an element out of view is refused, never tapped at stale bounds.
- `fill` tells the truth: verified through what the tree offers, unverified when it offers nothing, a mismatch only for a value that differs.
- Typed text lands whole on iOS from the first key.
- Every control an agent needs is listed once with a ref: tabs, checkboxes.
- Keep the core lean: each rule gated on the platform or shape that needs it (the drawing-order cover on iOS, the keyboard wait on iOS after a focusing tap, the descendant read-back for a field without a value of its own).

**Non-Goals:**
- Changing the device driver's tree (the physical-ios change); the fixes apply to the device through the shared mirror once its tree carries the attributes.
- Fixing the app's own accessibility (its code field names the digits).

## Decisions

### D31. Covers on iOS from the tree's drawing order
On iOS the reader hands the mirror the tree in XCTest's order, which is UIKit's subview order: a later sibling is drawn over an earlier one. The mirror applies the `on_top` rule of task 10.28 to iOS nodes without a flag: a node drawn after a sibling it overlaps, when it shows something (a node on screen with a ref or a name in it or below it), cuts what the sibling holds within its bounds; a node with no area is not judged, and only siblings are compared, as on Android. The shapes from the smoke test: a fixed footer's button over a heading (consent), a floating button over a list's last row (create), a sticky header over a scrolled card (feed). Alternative: an explicit flag from the Simulator's reader (rejected: XCTest's snapshot reports no occlusion and no drawing order beyond the children's order; the order is the information). Risk, as on Android: a transparent container drawn over content hides it; the Bluesky and the smoke test's screens showed none. `Mirror::covered_by` reports the cover, and `click` fails with `element_covered` naming the innermost control of the cover that a tap would hit, or `overlay` for a plain container, with advice to scroll the element out from under it rather than to act on the cover first, which on a consent screen would accept the consent. Two things task 1.1 found: the rule is gated on trees whose windows are rooted at `XCUIElementType` classes from a backend that is not a fallback (the translator flattens the order), and a tab bar or toolbar is never cut by a later sibling, since XCTest lists a navigation bar before the full-screen list its controller draws it over; a tab bar listed after a list does cover the rows wholly under it, as on Android. Overlapping siblings are found by a sweep over top edges, about one comparison per sibling. The refreshed microbenchmark baseline (a quiet run at abf42ed against the one at b724af0) puts the cost at 0.4 µs on a 51-node view (`visibility/row-list` 7.1 to 7.5 µs, `scroll_viewport/list` 7.1 to 7.5, `wait/holds-list` 7.3 to 7.7, 5 to 6% each), 1.4 µs on the Expo list's full listing (21.6 to 23.0), 5 µs on the SwiftUI 500-cell screen's (321.8 to 327.1, 1.6%) and 24 µs on the React Native one's (475.4 to 499.5, 5.1%), with one allocation and 1.6 to 4 KB more per listing of the SwiftUI and Bluesky screens; the interactive listings of the 500-cell screens stayed within 1%. Accepted as the price of not tapping through a footer; the small views pay it in the view's build, where the sweep runs for every window.

### D32. No tap at bounds that are not in view
`click` and the other ref actions compute the element's visibility first (task 10.15). Today `scroll --until` brings an element in with a stroke; `click` makes no stroke, and an element less than mostly in view is tapped with a warning. The smoke test's case was neither: the keyboard's appearance moved the submit button up, the replica still held its old bounds (no event, the frame-driven re-read not yet due), visibility saw 0% in view, and the tap went to the old center on the number pad. The rule: an element with 0% in view and no scroll container to bring it in gets one read of its window, cache cleared, then the action either taps the fresh bounds or fails with `element_offscreen`; the warning path stays for elements partly in view. The re-read is the targeted kind of 10.29, bounded by the read's own cost.

### D33. Read-back through descendants, unverified rather than wrong
`fill` reads the target back after the entry (10.23). A composite field, as a React Native code input built of six boxes, has no value of its own: its digits live in its descendants' texts or values. The read-back therefore takes the field's own value when it has one, else the concatenation of its descendants' values or texts in order, and compares that; when neither exists, the reply succeeds with `verified: false` and a reason (`no_value_to_read`), never `text_mismatch`, which stays for a value that differs after the retry. `type` is unchanged. The reply's `method` keeps `keys` and `direct`; the guide says `direct` is a paste on iOS and the set on Android, and that a paste shows iOS's banner and replaces the pasteboard.

### D34. Keyboard-ready typing on iOS
When `fill` taps the field to focus it and the keyboard window was not up before the tap, the driver waits until the mirror shows the keyboard window (the input-method window the reader presents, 10.21) or a bound passes (1 s, above S3's 1.2 s slide-in measured from the tap's first frame is not needed: the window appears in the tree before the animation ends; the task measures the bound) before the first key. A field already focused with the keyboard up pays nothing. Android is untouched: the agent's text set does not type. The cost is measured against the text-entry series on the Simulator (`reports/10.26-remeasure.md`'s 10.31 numbers) and recorded.

### D35. Listing corrections
A node whose role is `tab` (a Pressable with `accessibilityRole="tab"`, which XCTest reports as a generic with the `selected` trait) gets a ref as a labelled control, selected or not; a checkbox lists as `checkbox` with `[checked=…]` as a switch does; a text node whose only child is its own text is listed once; a scroll bar once; a heading not nested in itself. Each from a fixture of the smoke test's shape, counts only.

### D36. A wait that already holds answers at once
`wait` pumps the mirror before it evaluates, and a pump performs a read that is due; a wait whose condition holds in the replica therefore paid for the read (43 to 473 ms in the smoke test, 380 ms at the median). The predicate is evaluated against the replica first; only when it does not hold does the wait take the due read and go on as before. The replica is current within the poll interval by the mirror's own rules, so the answer is as fresh as a snapshot's.

### D37. The tap point is the centre of what a tap can reach
The acceptance run of task 2.2 tapped the full centre of elements that were only partly reachable: a heading whose lower half lay under a fixed footer's button (the tap pressed the button, declined the consent and signed the app out), a card 70% under a sticky header (the tap hit the header, with the warning "30% of it in view"), submit buttons 1 to 35% in view under the keyboard (the tap hit the keyboard), while only 0% in view was refused (D32). The rule: for an action on an element the mirror gives the element's reachable region, its bounds within the viewport minus every cover over it (D38), and the tap point is the centre of the largest reachable rectangle; when no reachable rectangle is at least 8 points on each side, the action fails with `element_covered` naming what a tap at the element's centre would hit (the innermost control of the cover, `overlay` for a plain container, `keyboard` for the keyboard), or `element_offscreen` when nothing is in the viewport (D32's single re-read first). The warning for a partly visible element stays, now beside a tap that reaches it. Every action path takes it: by ref, by id, by text selector (the acceptance's selector click pressed a button through a 12 point text under it), and `fill`'s focusing tap. `scroll --until` judges arrival by the same region, never calling an element visible that a cover hides (the acceptance saw `visible 1.0` for a control under a disabled button). Alternative: refusing every partly covered element (rejected: a card whose lower third shows is a legitimate target, as Playwright taps the visible part and only refuses when another element intercepts the point). What tasks 3.1 and 3.2 found: one function (`visibility::reach`) answers `Mirror::visibility`, `covered_by` and a ref's aim, and the daemon aims a selector's element without a ref at the same point; the 8-point floor is `min(8, extent)` on each side, so the heading's upper 11 points take its tap and the 12-point text with 3 points out from under its button is refused naming the button; `fraction` is the share a tap reaches; a cover across the element also cuts the visible bounds on the side the element fits in with the least movement, which a stroke that brings it in aims for; an element under 8 points with nothing named at its centre fails `element_offscreen`, there being no general hit test; and a wholly covered element is named at its centre as a partly covered one, which names round one's Android typeahead by its button rather than as an overlay.

### D38. Covers that show nothing, bars and the keyboard
D31 cuts only what a later sibling covers when the sibling shows something. Three covers the acceptance met escape it. A disabled control: UIKit hit-tests a disabled control like an enabled one and the touch goes nowhere, so a disabled button is a cover whatever it shows (the acceptance tapped a control under a disabled 56 point button with no warning, twice). A bar listed before the content it is drawn over: XCTest lists a navigation bar or a header window first, so the order rule never sees it as a cover; a node whose platform class is a bar (`XCUIElementTypeNavigationBar`, `XCUIElementTypeTabBar`, `XCUIElementTypeToolbar`) or a header the app pins (a sibling with a fixed frame at the top of a scrolling container, found as the sticky header of D31 is) covers what lies within its bounds regardless of order, gated on iOS trees as D31 is (the acceptance's 44 point control at y 2 behind a header stayed listed). The keyboard window (the input-method window of 10.21): for taps it is a cover, and for the listing an element wholly under it is left out of the interactive listing with the keyboard named in the hint, an element partly under it listed with its visible fraction (the acceptance's N4: elements under the keyboard listed, and submits under it tapped). Android keeps the `on_top` flag's rule; the keyboard rule applies to both platforms through the input-method window the mirror already knows (10.25, 10.32). What task 3.1 found: the acceptance's control under the disabled button lies 38 of its 48 points under it, 79%, its lower 10 points out from under, so it stays listed and that part takes the tap; the rule is checked on a disabled view over a control wholly within it, named a `disabled control` when it says nothing else. A pinned header is a view listed before a scrolling sibling that does not scroll, spans its width and reaches from its top or above to its top or below; in the acceptance's list the header ends where the list's frame starts, so the rows behind it lie outside the list's bounds and the hint counts three above, not six. The keyboard's bounds are what the drivers say it draws, Gboard's floating toolbar included, which the listing and a refusal's name take, while `visibility` cuts by the keyboard as by a bar.

### D39. Read-back through the name, refs across a label change, a wrapper's field
Three read-back gaps from the acceptance. The composite code field spells its digits in its accessibility name, not in a value or in descendants, so every `fill` answered `no_value_to_read` and a wrong code (the server's 400) went unreported: the read-back takes, after the value and the descendants (D33), the target's own name when it changed between the read before the entry and the read after it, `readFrom: "name"`; a name that did not change is a label and is never compared. A field whose label changes once it holds text lost its ref (`stale_ref` by ref before anything was typed, N2) and could not be read back by selector (`verified: null`, one of two holding wrong text, two characters missing): the replica keeps a node's ref across a change of its name when its role, identifier, parent and position among its siblings are unchanged, so the read-back finds the field it filled; measured against `wait/events-typing`'s allocations in the microbenchmarks. On a second visit to the code screen, the field's id matched a generic "that takes no text" (N1, 4 of 4) while a click on the id then a `type` worked: when `fill` or `type` resolves an id to a non-editable node with exactly one editable descendant, the descendant is the target, named in the reply. What task 3.3 found: a field's view that gains a counter at the focus took a new identity, so the replica pairs the siblings of a class and identifier that neither content nor an identified descendant tells apart by their position; a ref's place is the position alone, the replica pairing only the children of paired nodes, so a surviving element's parent stays the same; Bluesky's Forgot password? becoming Next one position up stays stale; a field without an area inside the wrapper takes the tap on the wrapper and the text as the field in focus; and `wait/events-typing` keeps its 1,061 allocations and 139,257 bytes.

### D40. Tabs by their row
D35 gives a ref to a labelled generic with the `selected` trait; the acceptance found the same tab without a ref while another tab was selected (0 of 2). XCTest puts `selected` only on the selected tab, so the rule is the row's: labelled generic siblings laid out in one row where one carries `selected` are all tabs, listed with role `tab` and refs, the selected one with its state. Gated on a sibling carrying `selected`; a row without one changes nothing. Task 3.4 took the row as the siblings that overlap the selected one over at least half the shorter height, none inside an actionable element; the feed's and the creation tab's tab bars list as tabs too.

### D41. Scroll strokes inside the reachable part of the container
With the keyboard up, `scroll up 300` started its stroke at (201, 119.5), on the fixed header, and moved nothing, and `scroll --until` gave up after two strokes with "content stopped moving" while the listing said six more items above (N3): 10.32 keeps strokes off the keyboard, and the uncovered area left was the header's. The stroke runs inside the scroll container's bounds minus its covers (D38: a pinned header, the keyboard); when the reachable height is under the stroke's minimum (120 points), `scroll` fails with `covered_by_keyboard` (10.32's code) advising to dismiss the keyboard, rather than stroking a bar. Task 3.2 took the container as the one that shows the most of what the keyboard leaves (`Mirror::scroll_container_in`), and the minimum along the scroll's axis where the area alone has that much (`STROKE_ROOM`).

### D42. Typed entry on React Native fields: a measured cadence
Typed entry on the Simulator lost keys in the acceptance: the address field's "@" in 2 of 15 runs (the paste retry repaired it), one digit of the code in 1 of 5 `type` runs, two characters in a name field whose read-back failed. The Simulator types through `dtuhidd` at 10.31's cadence, which the SwiftUI fixture takes whole; a React Native field takes keys through the JavaScript thread. The cadence for React Native fields is decided by a measurement on this session's own Simulator: the Expo fixture's username field and a label-changing field of the fake app's shape, 20 runs of the 36-character entry at the current cadence and at two slower ones, the drop rate and the entry time recorded in the task; the slowest cadence that drops nothing becomes the cadence for fields of React Native trees (the mirror tells them by 7.11's shapes), the others unchanged. `type` gains no read-back; the guide says `fill` verifies and `type` does not.

### D43. The name read-back compares what changed
Task 3.3 compared the entry against the field's whole name, and the acceptance's code field names itself "[code], Verification code, required": every fill ended in `text_mismatch` after typing and pasting a code that was right (task 3.7, 5.6 to 6.0 s a fill). D39 meant the part of the name the entry changed: the read-back takes the name before the entry and after it, removes their common prefix and suffix, strips separators (commas, spaces, dashes and the placeholder glyphs of empty boxes) from what is left and from the entry, and compares those; `readFrom: "name"` with `verified: true` when they match, `text_mismatch` naming the changed part when they differ, `no_value_to_read` when the name did not change. A name that only gains the entry's length, as a masked field's does, is a mismatch, as a masked value is compared by length (1.3). What task 4.1 found: the read-back cuts both names into words at separators (spaces, commas, dashes, the empty boxes' among them, and underscores) and takes off the words the two share at their start and at their end; a code entered over a code it starts as ("123456" over "123499") shares its first words with the name before, so the part takes in the shared words next to it when they spell the entry with it; a mismatch names the part as it reads, a part of bullets by their number, so a wrong code entered over a code it starts as is named by its last digits alone.

### D44. A non-text node that raises the keyboard takes text
On the second visit the code field is a generic with its identifier and no children (the app's hidden input is not in the tree, N1), so no editable descendant exists for D39's wrapper rule, and `fill` and `type` by id fail `invalid_argument`, while a click on it raises the keyboard and an untargeted `type` lands the code. The rule: when `fill` or `type` resolves an identifier to a node that takes no text and holds no editable descendant, the action taps it and waits up to 1 s for the keyboard window (D34's wait); if the keyboard comes, the text goes to the focused input untargeted, the read-back runs through the node's value, descendants or name (D43), and the reply names the node and says the keyboard carried the text; if no keyboard comes, `invalid_argument` as before. Gated on the keyboard's arrival, so a plain generic still refuses at once. What task 4.2 found: the node is one an identifier names that takes no text, holds no field (`Mirror::fields_within`) and has no ref; a labelled node without traits or children gets a ref under task 7.12's rule and goes the way of a ref, the driver tapping it and typing, so the fixture's node holds an empty view out of sight, the listings not saying why the acceptance's node had none. The tap goes where a tap reaches the node, the wait is for the keyboard's window on either platform, and a field the tree shows with the focus after the tap refuses as no keyboard does, the keyboard serving that field; the reply gives `ref: null` and a warning naming the node by its role and name.

### D45. Reach is clipped by scroll containers, and the listing marks every cover
A Paste button sat under a floating button with 10 points showing below it, but those 10 points lay outside its scroll view's visible bounds: the mirror counted them reachable (19%), tapped them and nothing opened (N6). The reachable part of an element (D37) is clipped by the visible bounds of every scroll container above it, as `scroll_viewport` already knows them, before the covers are taken away. And the interactive listing marks every partly reachable element with `visible=NN%` and leaves out every element with no reachable part, counting it in the hint, whatever covers it (a header, the status bar, a floating button, a card scrolled above the screen) rather than the keyboard alone (N7: the heading at 50%, the card at 30% and two controls behind a header listed unmarked). An action on an unmarked element stays refused as 3.1 refuses it; the mark tells the agent before it acts. What task 4.3 found: what a container shows (`Sight::shows`) is its bounds less the bars and less the side of each view across it that the container does not fit in, worked out once per container in a pass; the listing takes each element's share in one pass (`visibility::listed`), measuring only the elements a view lies over in part or that pass the edge of what their containers show, marks one partly under the keyboard with the less of its two shares, and counts one a tap reaches none of in its container's hint by the side of the container's middle it lies on, while one outside every scroll container goes uncounted. A scroll indicator covers nothing but its own copy, since the 30-point hit areas XCTest gives indicators left an alert's OK button 61%. The masked listings hold no tree of the creation tab, so the fixture's footer and its short row are invented to put the strip outside what the list shows. The interactive listings take one allocation more, two on the SwiftUI grid, and against the commit before in one window no benchmark runs more than 5% slower.

### D46. `scroll --until` that stops early
On the Create screen `scroll down --until` a button failed `element_not_found` "content stopped moving" after three strokes of 300 points while a plain `scroll down 300` right after moved the content by 300 (N5), and the reply gave no stroke start to tell why. The cause is found from the screen's shape (a floating button over the list's lower part, a footer, the strokes' region after D41) with a fixture test that reproduces the three strokes and the stop; the stroke runs where the plain scroll's does, and the failure reply names the stroke's start and end and what moved, so the next such case is diagnosable from the reply. What task 4.4 found: the cause was neither the strokes' region nor the arrival test but D37's view across the element: a stroke that brings an element in aims at the visible bounds such a view leaves, and of the disabled button those kept the band below it, where the control fits with the least movement (38 points down against 74 up), the list's frame running on under the footer, so the strokes pushed the content toward a top it cannot pass while the plain scroll's pushed it the other way. D45's clip, the footer cutting what the list shows at the button's bottom, leaves the band no room; and with nothing drawn below the button, a view across an element keeps only a side its scroll containers can carry it to, as their semantics say (`Carry`), every side when one of them says nothing. The failure names the last stroke's start and end (`data.from`, `data.to`) and what moved after it: the element by how many points which way, else the screen, else nothing. The fake stops after two strokes where the run took three, a touch on the device seemingly changing the tree with the scroll bars it shows; a list whose platform says nothing of the ways it scrolls still takes the side of least movement.

### D47. The first letter under auto-capitalization: a measured rule
A field that auto-capitalizes its first letter turned a typed "Smoke Org" into "smoke Org" four times out of four (task 3.7): iOS raises the keyboard's shift for the first letter, and the shifted key the driver sends lands lowercase, the retry then pasting the text right with the pasteboard's side effect. Whether the tree tells such a field apart is unknown (it shows no auto-capitalization attribute), so the rule is measured on this session's own Simulator with a throwaway field of each auto-capitalization setting (none, words, sentences, all characters): a capital first letter sent shifted and unshifted, 20 runs each, what lands recorded; then the smallest rule that types the first letter right for every setting without the retry (for example sending a capital first letter unshifted when the keyboard's shift state reads up, if the window tells it; or typing the first letter, reading it back and correcting it in place, cheaper than a paste) is applied, gated on the first letter of the entry being a capital, and the measurement recorded in the task. What task 4.5 found: the shift is not the cause. A key sent with Shift lands as a capital in every setting and one sent without it in lower case, whatever the keyboard shows, while the keyboard's window does show the shift (its `shift` key `selected`, its letter keys labelled in capitals); the capital is lost when `fill` types over what Command-A selected in the field it just tapped, as it does whenever the field holds text: the field shows "Smoke" until the space, then autocorrection writes "smoke " over it, in fields of every auto-capitalization setting (74 of 80 SwiftUI entries, 62 of 68 React Native ones) and in none without autocorrection. The window's shift does not tell it (the field without auto-capitalization was rewritten ten times out of ten with its shift down), nor does reading the first letter back after its key (it reads "S" until the space). The rule is the daemon's, which selects the field's text: for keys whose first letter is a capital, Command-A is followed by the delete key and the keys wait for a read that shows the field empty, at most 1 s, after which none of 160 entries was rewritten.

### D48. Scroll areas told by the platform's element type
The Create screen's tree, as the other session read it for task 4.7, is flat: Paste, the floating Generate button, the scroll bar texts and the tabs are siblings of the root generic, with no scroll view and no footer between them, so D45's clip had no container to clip by and the strip the scroll view hides still counted as reachable (N6), while `scroll --until` strokes in the window when no container is known. React Native's scroll view is a `UIScrollView` subclass under its own class name (`RCTScrollView` wrapping `RCTCustomScrollView`), and the Simulator reader tells scroll areas by what it reads of the platform class; the Expo fixture's list, whose rows the mirror scrolls, is found, so the difference lies in which classes or shapes are taken as scroll areas. The rule: a scroll area is told by the platform's element type as XCTest reports it (the `ScrollView`, `Table` and `CollectionView` types) and by the scroll traits of the platform's accessibility, never by a class name alone, so every `UIScrollView` subclass gives the mirror its scroll semantics and its visible bounds; the scroll bar texts stay inside it. Reproduced on this session's own Simulator with a throwaway React Native screen of the Create shape (a scroll view whose frame ends above a footer holding a floating button that overlaps the list's last row), the fix is in the reader or the conversion, wherever the class name is read, and the mirror's clip then applies. Alternative: inferring the scroll view from the scroll bar texts' extent (rejected: the bars are the view's children only when the view is known). What task 5.1 found: the premise did not hold. The reader tells a scroll area by the runtime's automation type already (`elements::is_scroll_view`: ScrollView, Table, CollectionView), and React Native's scroll view is an `XCUIElementTypeScrollView` inside `RCTScrollViewComponentView`, as the Expo fixture's `FlatList` is, the SwiftUI fixture's `List` an `XCUIElementTypeCollectionView`; the listing is flat because the full listing skips unnamed containers, the scroll view among them, and the run's hint after the scroll bars came from the scroll view's semantics. A throwaway screen on this session's Simulator gave the run's boxes, listings, tap and stroke to the point with a scroll view that runs on under the footer to the bottom of the screen, not one whose frame ends above it, which the mirror already clipped (D45). The cause is React Native's accessibility: a view that draws something, as the footer's background, is listed as a node that holds nothing, what it lays out listed after it as its siblings, top to bottom, and a view that draws nothing is not listed at all (the throwaway's transparent, `box-none` and layout-only footers left no node), so the footer showed nothing and D31's later-sibling rule, which needs a cover that shows something, took it for no cover. The rule: in XCTest's trees a view that holds nothing and names nothing, without a ref or an identifier, listed after a sibling that holds or is a scroll container it lies over part of, covers what it lies over, as a later sibling that shows something does; one that holds all of the scroll container, as a background listed after a list would, covers none of it. What the scroll view shows then ends at the footer's top, the row's strip has no reachable part, and `scroll --until`'s strokes, three quarters of what the list shows, keep clear of the floating button. The reader needed no change.

### D49. The read-back's reference reading is taken after the field is emptied
A refill of the same code answered `no_value_to_read` (N8): the name read-back compares the name before the entry with the name after, and a field refilled with what it held has the same name both times. Task 4.5 already empties a field holding text before keys with a capital first letter (`fill` selects the text and deletes it, then waits for a read showing the field empty); that rule now applies to every `fill` typed as keys into a field that holds text, and the reference reading for the read-back (value, descendants or name) is the one taken after the emptying, so that the entry is compared with an empty start. A field that holds nothing is untouched; a paste keeps its path. Cost: the 0.28 s task 4.5 measured, on refills only. What task 5.2 found: the emptying is a step of `fill` itself, so that the mirror reads the emptied field: an empty entry into the field (the Simulator selects what it holds and deletes it), a wait of at most 1 s for the replica to show the field empty, and the keys then into the field the emptying gave the focus, the read-back comparing with the field's name as the emptying left it. A field holds text when its own value or what lies below it shows some; a field known only by its name holds text, as far as the tree tells, when the name spells the entry already, the one case where keys over it leave the name as it was, so a first fill of a code field, whose name spells dashes, is untouched, as task 4.1's test has it. Only a Simulator types keys over a field's text (a physical iPhone's runner sets the text of a field that holds some, Android sets it), so the step is the Simulator's, keys deciding as `simulator::TextMethod::resolve` does. The Simulator's input readies the field in focus as one it taps, Command-A first, so text `fill` enters without naming the field (after the emptying, and for an identifier on a node that holds no field) replaces what the field holds rather than going after it. On the Expo fixture's username field a refill took 0.93 s at p50 against 0.665 s before, a first fill 0.655 s against 0.66, and the code field's refill of the code it held, unverifiable before, verified through its name.

### D50. The full listing marks and counts as the interactive one does
The interactive listing marks `visible=NN%` and leaves out what a tap cannot reach, counting it in the hint (D45), but the full listing marks only the keyboard's cover (N9), and its hint lines, which the interactive listing prints as they are, do not count what the interactive listing dropped (N7): "(6 more actionable items above…)" leaves out the two controls behind the header. The full listing marks every partly reachable element as the interactive one does and keeps every element, since it is the full listing; the interactive listing's hint lines count what it dropped for a cover beside what is scrolled away, in words that say which ("2 behind the header", "under the keyboard"), so the counts agree with the listing they head. ## Delivery process

The archived design's Delivery process binds this change unchanged. Fixtures are synthetic shapes with placeholder names, never the app's content; the acceptance run is the other session's, on its Simulator and app, reported as counts and outcomes.

## Risks / Trade-offs

- [A transparent later sibling on iOS hides what it spans] → the rule needs the cover to show something, as on Android; a screen that hits it is recorded and the rule gated further.
- [The re-read before a refused tap costs a read on every click of a partly visible element] → only 0% in view triggers it; partly visible elements keep the warning path.
- [The keyboard wait adds up to its bound on fields whose keyboard never comes (a field with its own custom input)] → the bound is short and the wait skipped when the keyboard was up before the tap; measured in task 1.4.

## Migration Plan

Behavioural fixes behind the same commands and codes; `fill` replies gain `verified: false` with a reason where they failed before. Nothing to deploy.
